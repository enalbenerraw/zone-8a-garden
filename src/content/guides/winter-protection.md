A Zone 8a winter bed survives most nights on its own. The crops in the [fall and winter calendar](../planting-calendar/) were chosen because they handle frost, and several taste better for it. Protection matters on the handful of nights each winter that drop well below freezing, and on the first hard freeze after a warm spell. This guide covers what each crop can take, what to do on a freeze night, and what to do with beds that are already empty.

## How cold can each crop take?

Frost begins at 32°F. Below that, damage depends on both **how cold** it gets and **how long** it stays there, so treat every number below as a range, not a line (NC State Extension). Sources disagree for some crops, and the table shows the spread instead of picking one.

| Group | Crops | Where damage starts | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Least hardy | Cauliflower, head and leaf lettuce, arugula, Florence fennel | Upper 20s °F. Harvest cauliflower before a 28°F night (NC State). Fennel is listed at 30°F (Oregon State). | Harvest ahead of a forecast in the mid to upper 20s, or cover |
| Hardy | Broccoli, cabbage | Tolerate brief dips into the mid to upper 20s with some leaf damage (NC State); listed for moderate freezes of 24 to 28°F (South Dakota State) | Cover below the mid 20s; harvest mature heads first |
| Hardiest | Brussels sprouts, kale, collards, spinach | Survive longer spells below 26°F (NC State). Clemson reports Brussels sprouts taking 20°F with no damage. | Harvest or cover when the low 20s are imminent (UGA); otherwise leave them be |

Two details move these numbers:

- **Acclimation.** Plants harden to freezing weather when it arrives gradually. A warm spell partway through winter can undo that hardening, so a hard freeze right after a mild week hits harder than the same freeze in a steady cold stretch (Texas A&M AgriLife).
- **Spinach is the crop sources disagree on most.** NC State and Oregon State rate it among the hardiest, while UGA and South Dakota State group it with lettuce. Mulched, covered spinach overwinters well; leaves you plan to eat soon are safer picked ahead of a hard freeze.
- **Sweetening.** As temperatures fall, plants convert stored carbohydrates into sugars that act as a natural antifreeze inside their cells (Cornell Cooperative Extension). This is why spinach, kale, and collards taste sweeter after frost, and why a light frost improves Brussels sprouts (University of Illinois Extension).

## Which row cover weight should you use?

Row cover is the main winter tool. It holds in the heat rising from the soil overnight. Heavier fabric holds more heat but lets through less light, so a heavy cover belongs on for a cold snap, not all winter.

| Fabric weight | Degrees of protection | Light let through | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lightweight (about 0.5 oz/sq yd) | About 2 to 4°F | About 85% | Frost down to about 28°F (Clemson); can stay on for days |
| Heavyweight (about 1.5 to 2 oz/sq yd) | About 4 to 10°F | About 30 to 50% | Hard-freeze nights; take off or open once the cold passes |

Ranges are drawn from Oregon State, University of New Hampshire, and New Mexico State extension publications, which test different fabrics and disagree on exact numbers. Fall covering can protect a little more than these figures, because autumn soil holds more heat (New Mexico State). Two layers of fabric add protection on a severe night, and a lightweight cover alone gives little to no protection in a hard freeze (Texas A&M AgriLife).

## What to do on a freeze night

When the forecast low drops into the mid 20s or below:

1. **Harvest what will not hold.** Cut mature cauliflower and broccoli heads and lettuce first (see the next section).
2. **Water the soil the day before.** Moist soil stores more heat and releases it slowly overnight (NC State; Texas A&M AgriLife).
3. **Cover before sundown,** late in the afternoon or early evening, so the cover traps the day's stored ground heat (Texas A&M AgriLife).
4. **Hold the cover off the leaves.** Use hoops 16 to 20 inches high. Where fabric rests on leaves, those leaves can still freeze (Clemson; Colorado State; University of New Hampshire).
5. **Weight the edges to the ground.** Bury or weight the sides so the warm air stays in (Colorado State; University of New Hampshire).
6. **Open up the next day.** Remove or open heavy covers once temperatures rise, so the sun can recharge the soil and plants do not overheat (Colorado State; NC State).

Skip clear plastic laid directly on plants. It cools fast at night and damages leaves where it touches them, and plastic left on into a sunny day can cook the crop underneath (Texas A&M AgriLife; University of Nebraska-Lincoln).

## What to harvest before a hard freeze

- **Leaf lettuce, head lettuce, and spinach** that you want to eat soon: pick when the mid to upper 20s are forecast (UGA).
- **Cauliflower:** cut mature heads before a 28°F night. It is the most cold-sensitive crop in the bed, and the plant makes no side shoots after the head is cut, so pull it once harvested (NC State).
- **Broccoli:** cut the main head when it is ready, then leave the plant. It keeps producing side shoots until cold shuts it down (NC State).
- **Brussels sprouts:** pick from the bottom of the stalk up once sprouts reach 1 to 1.5 inches (Clemson). Cutting off the top of the plant brings the harvest forward but can lower the total yield (University of Illinois Extension).
- **Kale and collards:** take a few of the lower leaves from each plant at a time, and leave the growing center to keep producing through winter (NC State).

## How to carry spinach through to spring

The fall-sown Bloomsdale spinach on the south border is meant to overwinter and be the first harvest of the next spring.

- Aim for plants with **four to five true leaves** going into winter; that size overwinters best (Utah State).
- **Mulch around the plants** to protect the soil and roots. Recommendations range from 3 to 6 inches (NC State) up to 8 inches where freeze and thaw cycles can heave plants out of the soil (Virginia Tech).
- **Add row cover** over overwintering spinach, kale, and lettuce for the coldest stretch (NC State).

## What to do with beds that are already empty

Bare soil over winter loses structure and nutrients. Either grow a cover crop or mulch it.

| Option | Sowing window | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cereal rye | Mid September to mid October preferred; possible to about November 15 (NC State, Piedmont) | The only common cover crop still in its window by mid November |
| Crimson clover | August 25 to October 1 (NC State, Piedmont) | Fixes nitrogen; plan it for next fall |
| Hairy vetch | August 25 to October 15 (NC State, Piedmont) | More winter hardy than clover; fixes nitrogen |
| Oats | September to October (Clemson) | Usually winter-killed; leaves an easy mulch |
| Mulch only | Any time | 3 to 6 inches of straw or leaves protects the soil if you skip a cover crop (NC State) |

Late-sown cover crops carry more risk of winterkill (NC State), so after mid November, mulch is the safer choice. Pair this with the [succession and soil method](../succession-sequence/) and the [organic fertilizing plan](../organic-fertilizing/) when the bed comes back into use in spring.

## Is it too late to plant garlic?

Not yet in most of Zone 8a. NC State gives a planting window of **late October through November**, possibly into December farther south. Cloves need four to six weeks to root before the ground gets cold, and go 2 to 3 inches deep. Clemson recommends 4 to 6 inches of weed-free straw mulch over the planting in late fall. Clemson's chart gives an earlier window for the South Carolina Piedmont, so check your own extension office's dates.

> These thresholds and timings come from university extension publications, which test in different climates and do not always agree. Use them as a framework, watch your own forecast, and confirm product and timing details with your local extension office.
