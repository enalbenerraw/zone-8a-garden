Pest pressure runs all year in Zone 8a, but it is not even. Each season brings its own pests, and one moment stands above the rest: the **August brassica transplant window**, when young brassicas going into warm soil are exactly what cabbage moths, loopers, aphids, and flea beetles are looking for. The plan is the same in every season, preventative and layered, set up before the damage starts rather than after.

## Pests by season

| Season | Crops at risk | Main pests | First line of defense |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring | Greens, radishes, brassica seedlings | Flea beetles, aphids, cutworms | Row cover on seedlings; trap crops; soap for aphids |
| Summer | Beans, squash, tomatoes, peppers | Squash bugs, bean beetles, hornworms, aphids, spider mites | Handpicking, Bt for caterpillars, soap for soft-bodied pests |
| Fall and winter | Brassicas | Cabbage moths and loopers, aphids, flea beetles | Row cover at transplant, weekly Bt, trap crops |

The same four tools cover every row of that table: physical exclusion (row cover), a biological caterpillar control (Bt), trap crops and companions, and an insecticidal soap for soft-bodied pests. The sections below work through them at their hardest test, the brassica window, then note where each one carries into the other seasons.

## Layer 1: Row covers (physical exclusion)

Floating row cover is the first and most reliable defense. Put it over the brassica beds **at transplant** and keep it on through establishment. It is a physical barrier: moths cannot land and lay eggs on what they cannot reach. The same cover protects spring brassica seedlings and young greens from flea beetles; in summer it goes over squash and cucumbers until they flower, then comes off so bees can pollinate.

- Use lightweight insect netting or spun-bonded row cover.
- Secure the edges fully; a gap is an invitation.
- Lift it only to inspect, weed, or harvest, then close it back up.

Because these fall brassicas are not relying on insect pollination, you can leave the cover on for most of the crop's life.

## Layer 2: Bt spray schedule (biological control)

For the caterpillars that get through, *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) is a targeted biological control. It affects leaf-eating caterpillars and leaves everything else alone. Bt is a live microbial product you buy, not something you can brew at home: it is cultured and standardized commercially, then sold as an OMRI-listed spray for organic growing. Several Bt products carry a 0-day pre-harvest interval, so check the label.

- **Start weekly applications around August 22**, as the first transplants establish and cover comes off for inspection.
- Spray late in the day; Bt breaks down in direct sunlight.
- Reapply after rain, and rotate inspection with spraying so you are treating actual pressure, not spraying blind.
- A dedicated **1-gallon pump sprayer** kept for this purpose keeps the weekly rhythm easy.

The same Bt works on leaf-eating caterpillars all year: tomato hornworms in summer respond to it just as cabbageworms do in fall. Match the application to the pest you actually see, and keep handpicking large hornworms, which is faster than spraying for a few big caterpillars.

## Layer 3: Trap crops and companions (behavioral)

The [bed layout](../bed-layout/) already builds in the third layer. Nasturtiums and dill in the mid canopy pull aphids and caterpillars toward themselves and away from the brassicas, and the thyme and sage beside the anchors mask the scent that brassica moths home in on. Mustard greens on the south border double as a flea-beetle trap.

## Making your own: insecticidal soap for aphids

If you want a spray you can mix yourself, there is exactly one that extension services endorse for this bed, and it is for aphids, not caterpillars. A dilute soap solution kills soft-bodied pests (aphids, mites, whiteflies) on contact by breaking down their outer surface.

Mix an **insecticidal soap at roughly 2 percent**:

- 5 tablespoons of pure liquid soap per gallon of water (Colorado State University Extension), or
- 4 teaspoons of pure liquid soap per quart of water (University of California IPM).

Use **pure soap**, such as liquid castile, not dish detergent. Detergents and degreasers strip the waxy protective layer off the leaves and damage the plant.

Precautions that keep it safe on edible crops:

- Stay at or below 2 percent. Above 3 percent injures most plants, and sensitive plants can be hurt at 1.5 percent (Michigan State University Extension). Test on a few leaves first and wait a day if you are unsure.
- Soap works only while wet and only on insects it directly touches. There is no lasting residue, so coverage is everything: hit the undersides of leaves where aphids cluster, and repeat as the population rebuilds.
- Spray in the cool part of the day, and skip plants that are heat- or drought-stressed.
- Rinse harvested leaves before eating.

Soap does **not** control caterpillars. The imported cabbageworm and cabbage looper are largely immune to it. For those, rely on the row covers and Bt above, or another OMRI-listed product such as spinosad (the strongest of the group), neem, or pyrethrum (University of Maryland Extension).

## What to skip

Extension services specifically warn against several popular home brews on food crops:

- **Dish soap and detergent sprays.** They strip the leaf's waxy cuticle and do more harm than the pests (University of California ANR).
- **Tobacco sprays.** Nicotine is highly poisonous and has no place on edible plants.
- **Garlic, hot pepper, vinegar, and herbal mixes.** They lack consistent evidence of working, and homemade pesticides on food crops have no established safety tolerance. Penn State Extension notes that produce treated with unregistered remedies cannot legally be sold.

When soap, row covers, and Bt are not enough, reach for a labeled, OMRI-listed product rather than a kitchen recipe.

## Putting the brassica window on the calendar

The fall brassica window is the peak, so it gets the tightest schedule. Spring and summer crops use the same toolkit on a lighter cadence: cover seedlings early, scout weekly, and reach for Bt or soap only when you see the pest.

| Timing | Action |
|---|---|
| At transplant (August 8 to 22) | Row cover goes on over every brassica |
| From August 22 | Begin weekly Bt applications; inspect under cover |
| Through fall | Reapply Bt after rain; keep trap crops in place |
| First frost onward | Pressure drops sharply; relax the schedule |

Cold is your ally at the end. Once hard frosts arrive, pest pressure falls off and the crops, especially kale and collards, only get sweeter.

> These protocols are reconstructed for general Zone 8a use. Confirm product labels and local extension guidance before applying any spray, and adjust timing to your own transplant dates.
