A high-rotation bed grows three or more crops off the same soil in a year, so the soil has to be fed back as fast as the crops draw it down. The organic approach is to feed the soil, not the plant: build steady organic matter and biological activity, and let that release nutrients at the pace roots can use. This guide covers the whole year. The [succession and soil method](../succession-sequence/) covers how it threads through the specific spinach-beans-brassicas relay.

## The base: compost and organic matter

Everything starts with compost. A bed worked this hard wants a steady return of organic matter, not a one-time dose.

- **Top up with finished compost** at every crop changeover. A one to two inch layer worked into the top few inches resets organic matter and feeds soil life.
- **Keep the soil covered.** Mulch between plantings holds moisture, moderates soil temperature, and breaks down into more organic matter.
- **Return what the bed grows.** Trimmed tops, spent leaves, and chopped cover crops go back into the soil or the compost pile rather than the trash.

## The amendment method: a shallow manure hybrid

When you add aged manure, the method matters as much as the material. Use the hybrid approach the [succession method](../succession-sequence/) lays out:

1. **Top-dress** the bed with well-aged manure.
2. **Work it into only the top 2 to 3 inches** of soil.

Top-dressing alone leaves nutrients sitting on the surface; tilling deep risks burning young roots on hot manure. Working it in shallowly puts the nutrients where new roots find them while keeping the root zone safe. Only ever use **well-aged or composted** manure on a bed in active rotation. Fresh manure is too strong and carries a food-safety risk on crops you eat raw.

## The legume green-manure handoff

The cheapest nitrogen in this system is the nitrogen the bed grows itself. Bush beans, the summer relay crop, are a legume: they fix nitrogen from the air into root nodules. When the beans finish, **cut the foliage at the soil line and drop it in place as green manure** rather than pulling it. The nitrogen-rich tops break down fast and feed the heavy-feeding brassicas that transplant on top of them. Leaving the roots in the soil keeps the fixed nitrogen in the bed.

This is the single most important feeding event in the year-round plan: it is what lets the brassicas inherit a bed that is ready for them instead of one that needs rescuing.

## A year-round feeding cadence

| Season | What the bed needs | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Late winter to spring | Light feeding for greens and roots | Work in compost before sowing; greens and roots are modest feeders and need little more |
| Spring to summer | Soil rebuilding | Let the legume crop fix nitrogen; side-dress fruiting crops (tomatoes, peppers) with compost as they set fruit |
| Late summer | Heavy feeding ahead | Chop-and-drop the beans, top-dress with aged manure, and work in compost before the brassicas go in |
| Fall to winter | Sustained release for brassicas | Side-dress hungry brassicas once mid-season; mulch to protect soil life through the cold |

## Match the feeding to the crop

- **Heavy feeders** (brassicas, especially cabbage and broccoli; tomatoes; squash) want the richest bed: compost plus the manure amendment plus a mid-season side-dressing.
- **Light feeders** (lettuce, spinach, radishes, carrots, beets) want steady but modest fertility. Over-feeding leafy greens with nitrogen can push soft growth that pests prefer.
- **Legumes** (beans, peas, southern peas) feed themselves and the next crop. Do not add nitrogen for them; let them do their job and leave their roots in the bed.

## When in doubt, test

Chop-and-drop and compost are the low-effort, soil-first path, and they work. If you would rather not guess, a **soil test before the heavy feeders go in** tells you exactly where nitrogen and pH stand and removes the doubt. A local extension office runs them cheaply. Heavy feeders reward the certainty.

> This feeding plan is reconstructed from standard organic and Zone 8a practice, not a lab-calibrated program. Treat it as a sound framework, confirm rates and materials against your local extension office, and let a soil test settle anything you are unsure of.
