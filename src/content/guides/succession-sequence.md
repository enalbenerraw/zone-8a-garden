The whole rotation rests on three clean handoffs. Each crop leaves the soil in a specific state, and the next crop is chosen to use what the last one left or to repair what it took.

## Stage 1: Spring spinach comes out

When spinach bolts in late spring, do not pull the roots. **Trim the plants to the soil line** and leave the root mass in place to aerate and decompose. Spinach is a moderate nitrogen user, so the bed comes out of spring a little depleted but with intact soil structure.

## Stage 2: Summer bush beans rebuild the bed

Bush beans are the interim June to August crop, and they earn their place by fixing nitrogen. As a legume, they pull nitrogen from the air and fix it in root nodules, replenishing what the spinach drew down and priming the bed for the heavy-feeding brassicas to come.

Sow bean seed at **1 inch deep** directly into the amended bed. They need no transplanting and little fuss through the summer.

### The soil amendment: a hybrid mix

Before or as the beans go in, amend with manure using a **hybrid method** rather than either extreme:

1. **Top-dress** the bed with well-aged manure.
2. **Till it into only the top 2 to 3 inches** of soil.

Top-dressing alone leaves nutrients sitting on the surface; tilling deep risks burning young roots on hot manure. Working it shallowly integrates the nutrients where new roots will find them while keeping the root zone safe.

## Stage 3: Chop-and-drop into the brassicas

In early August, the beans give the bed one last gift. Rather than pulling them, **cut the foliage at the soil line and drop it in place as green manure** around the first week of August (roughly August 1 to 5). The nitrogen-rich bean tops break down fast and feed the brassicas you transplant on top of them.

The brassica transplants then go in on the schedule in the [planting calendar](../planting-calendar/), into a bed that has been fed twice: once by the manure amendment, once by the bean residue.

## A judgment call worth making

Chop-and-drop is the low-effort, soil-first path, and it works. If you would rather not guess, a **soil test before the brassicas go in** tells you exactly where nitrogen and pH stand and removes the doubt. Heavy feeders reward the certainty. Either path is defensible; the test simply trades a few days for confidence.

Once the brassicas are in, your attention shifts to keeping the pests off them. That is the [pest management plan](../pest-management/).
